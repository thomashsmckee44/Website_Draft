Design for manufacture, usually shortened to DFM, is the practice of designing a product so that it can be made well, repeatably, and at a sensible cost by the process that will actually produce it. It is one of those terms that gets used loosely, so it is worth being precise: DFM is not a step you do at the end to tidy a design up. It is a way of designing from the start, with the manufacturing process in mind, so that the choices you make are the ones that pay off when you are making thousands rather than one.

I put this near the front of everything I do, because it is where most of a product's unit cost is quietly decided. By the time a factory sends you a quote, the majority of that number is already locked in by decisions made months earlier in CAD. A factory can shave a little off the edges, but it cannot rescue a design that was expensive to make by its very shape. That is why DFM matters so much, and why doing it after the design is frozen is close to useless.

## Why the design decides the cost, not the factory

It is tempting to think of cost as something you negotiate with a supplier. In reality, the design sets the ceiling and the floor. The number of parts, the processes each one needs, the materials, the tolerances you have asked for, the finishing operations, and how it all goes together: these are all engineering decisions, and every one of them has a price that gets multiplied by your production volume.

A single unnecessary part might add a few pence in material and a few seconds of assembly. Multiply that across a production run and it is real money, every unit, forever. Ask for a tolerance ten times tighter than the product needs and you can double the cost of a part for no benefit the customer will ever notice. Design a moulded part that cannot release cleanly from a tool and you force expensive tooling, slow cycle times, or costly redesign after the tool is cut. None of that is the factory's doing. It is baked into the geometry.

## The principles that actually matter

DFM is not a single rule, it is a set of habits. These are the ones that make the biggest difference in practice.

**Reduce the part count.** Every part is a thing to design, tool, buy, store, inspect and assemble, and every join between two parts is a chance for something to fit badly. Combining functions into fewer, cleverer parts is usually the single largest cost lever on a product. The cheapest part is the one you designed out.

**Design for the process you will really use.** Injection moulding, sheet metal, die casting, machining and 3D printing each reward completely different geometry. A shape that is cheap to machine can be impossible to mould. Good DFM means committing early to the likely process and then designing to its grain: uniform wall thicknesses and generous draft angles for moulding, bend reliefs and simple flat patterns for sheet metal, and so on.

**Ask for the tolerances you need, and no tighter.** Precision is expensive, and it is expensive in a non-linear way. Every tolerance on a drawing is an instruction to the factory about how hard to work, so a tolerance should be there because the function genuinely needs it, and left relaxed everywhere else. Knowing where to be tight and where to be relaxed is a large part of the craft.

**Design out the assembly problems.** Parts that can only go together one way, that self-locate, that do not need a third hand or a special jig, that reduce the number of separate fasteners: all of this cuts labour and cuts the rate of units built wrong. A design that assembles smoothly on a line is worth a great deal more than one that only a patient engineer can put together on a bench.

**Avoid secondary operations where you can.** Every extra step after the main process, a machining pass, a coating, a hand-finish, a bit of rework, adds cost and adds a place for quality to drift. Sometimes they are worth it. Often a small design change removes the need for them entirely.

**Design for consistent quality across the whole run.** The goal is ten thousand units that are all acceptable, rather than one beautiful first sample. That means understanding how variation stacks up across parts, and designing so the product still works at the edges of the tolerances you allowed, rather than only when everything is made perfectly.

## Where DFM goes wrong

The most common failure is leaving it too late. A design gets developed, admired, sometimes even shown to customers, and only then handed over with a hopeful "can you make this cheaper". By that point the expensive decisions are set, and the honest answer is usually "not much, without changing the design you have fallen in love with".

The second failure is subtler, and it is the one I care about most. Plenty of people who present DFM as a service are really industrial designers or product designers who understand form and user experience well, but have never taken a product through tooling and volume production themselves. They can make something look manufacturable. Whether it truly is, whether the tool will run, whether the tolerances stack up, whether the assembly holds together at rate, is a different question, and it is answered by having been on the factory floor when things were being made, and having seen where designs fail in production. That gap is exactly where founders get hurt, because the problems do not appear until money has already been spent on a tool.
