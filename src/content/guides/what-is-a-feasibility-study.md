A feasibility study is the technical version of validating a business idea. Before you commit real time and money to developing a product, it answers a simple question in writing: can this actually be built, how, at what rough cost, and what are the risks. It is usually the smartest first spend on a new product, because it replaces optimism with evidence while changing course is still cheap.

## What a good feasibility study actually delivers

The core deliverable is a written report you can act on, brief a team with, or take to investors. A thorough one covers:

- **A technology assessment**: the quick engineering maths, and an honest review of what is already out there.
- **A developed project brief**, turning your idea and context into a clear, professional description of what you are building.
- **Routes to manufacture**, with indicative costs for each, so you can see how it would actually be made.
- **Design directions**, again with indicative costs, so the trade-offs are on the table.
- **Material selection**, the sensible options and why.
- **A trade-off analysis**, so you can see how each decision affects the brief.
- **A clear recommendation**: a straight go, no-go, or go-with-changes call, and a roadmap for the next steps.

The value is not a verbal "yeah, that should be fine". It is a document that lets you make your next decision with your eyes open.

## When your idea needs one

You probably need a feasibility study if you have caught yourself asking any of these:

- "Can this actually be made, or am I about to chase something impossible?"
- "What will it realistically cost and how long will it take to develop?"
- "What are the technical risks with a project like this, and how would I solve them?"

If the honest answer to any of them is "I do not know yet", that uncertainty is exactly what a study is designed to remove, early, for a few hundred pounds, rather than after months of development.

## When you might not

If your concept is already proven and built, and what you actually need is to get it ready for volume manufacture or to verify it before tooling, then a feasibility study is not the right starting point, and a [design verification](/services/design-verification) or [manufacturing preparation](/services/manufacturing-preparation) engagement fits better. A good practitioner will tell you this honestly rather than sell you a study you do not need.

## What happens if the answer is "no"

The best-case outcome of a study is sometimes that you find out, quickly and cheaply, that the idea will not work in its current form. That sounds like bad news, but finding out now, before a tooling commitment, is the entire point, and you still get the full report, the reasons why, and wherever possible alternative directions worth exploring. Usually there are some.

## The bigger picture

A feasibility study stands on its own, and the report is yours to use however you like. For many founders it is also the opening phase of [full product development](/services/product-development), where the same idea is carried all the way through to a product that is ready to manufacture. Because the study de-risks the idea and prices what follows, moving into the next phase is straightforward when you are ready.

If you have an idea you have been circling, a study is the cleanest way to get a straight answer before you invest. That is what a [feasibility study](/services/feasibility-study) is for.
