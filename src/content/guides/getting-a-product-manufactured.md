There is a large and expensive gap between a design that works and a design a factory can build a thousand times a week, economically and repeatably. Crossing it well is its own engineering discipline, and it is where a lot of first-time hardware products quietly lose money. This guide covers how a finished design becomes a manufactured product, and the decisions that decide whether it is cheap or painful to make.

## "Cheaper" is the wrong target. Lifetime production cost is the right one

Founders often think of manufacturing cost as the unit price a factory quotes. That is only part of it. The number that actually matters is the lifetime production cost: cycle time, assembly labour, tooling maintenance, scrap and warranty, all of which compound at medium and high volume. A part that is a few pence cheaper but slower to assemble, or that scraps more often, can cost far more over a production run. Designing those costs out early, deliberately, is where the real and repeated savings are.

## The routes to manufacture, and their trade-offs

How a product is made shapes how it must be designed, so the process is chosen early, not late. Injection moulding, pressure die casting, sheet-metal pressing, deep drawing, machining: each has its own rules for wall thickness, draft, radii and features, and each has a different balance of tooling cost against unit cost. High tooling cost with low unit cost suits high volume; the reverse suits low volume. Getting this choice right, and designing for the chosen process from the start, is what a [feasibility study](/services/feasibility-study) prices at the very beginning, and what a full development engagement carries through.

## What "design for manufacture" actually means

"Design for manufacture" (DFM), and its partner "design for assembly" (DFA), get listed casually on a lot of services pages, but the discipline is specific. It means refining a design so it is genuinely easy, cheap and reliable to make and put together: fewer parts, simpler processes, features that suit the tool, tolerances that hold across a whole production run rather than just on the first golden sample, and assemblies that cannot be built wrong. Real DFM comes from having taken products through tooling and volume production and seen where they go wrong, not from a checklist.

This is the core of [manufacturing preparation](/services/manufacturing-preparation): a DFMA review, detail production CAD, a full manufacturing drawing pack with GD&T, and a tolerance stack analysis so parts fit and function every time.

## Tolerances, and designing for quality at volume

A prototype fits together because you made one and adjusted it. A production run of tens of thousands has to fit together without anyone adjusting anything, across all the natural variation in moulding, cooling and assembly. That is what a tolerance stack analysis is for: it treats the variation statistically and predicts the fraction of assemblies that would be too tight or too loose, so you can spend tolerance precisely where it matters and open everything else up. It turns "it should be fine" into a number you can design against.

## Working with a manufacturer

Preparing the design is one half; working with the factory is the other. That means a complete, unambiguous documentation pack a supplier can quote and build from without guessing, and someone who can speak the language of manufacture on both sides through tooling, first articles and the inevitable ramp-up issues. Sourcing the supplier itself is a separate skill, but preparing the design so it is ready to hand over, and acting as the technical liaison with the manufacturer you choose in the UK or overseas, is exactly what turns a design into a made product.

## Where this fits in the journey

Manufacturing is the last stage of the path from idea to shelf. It assumes a design that is functionally proven, so if yours is not there yet, [design verification](/services/design-verification) or [concept and MVP development](/services/concept-mvp-development) comes first. If you would rather have one engineer carry the product the whole way, this is a phase of [full product development](/services/product-development).

If you have a design that works but is not yet ready to build at volume, that is precisely the gap [manufacturing preparation](/services/manufacturing-preparation) exists to close.
